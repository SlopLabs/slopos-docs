Plug a USB keyboard, mouse or tablet into a SlopOS machine and it types and
moves the cursor alongside the built-in keyboard and touchpad. Plug in a USB
stick and it becomes a disk, `sda`, that you can mount. Plug in a USB Ethernet
adapter and it becomes a network interface, `eth1`, with an address from
DHCP. Pull any of them out and the system lets go: the keys it held come up,
its disk or interface disappears, and nothing waits for it to answer.
Programs never talk to USB devices directly: a device shows up only as the
input, disk or network interface its kind gives it. What you don't get is everything else USB
carries, from audio and cameras to Bluetooth and printers; the
[list is below](#what-usb-devices-slopos-doesnt-support).

Every part follows a published specification: Intel's *eXtensible Host
Controller Interface* (xHCI) for the controller, and the USB Implementers
Forum's documents for the rest: USB 2.0 and 3.2 for the bus and hubs, HID 1.11
for keyboards and pointers, the Bulk-Only Transport for sticks, and the
Communications Device Class (CDC) with its ECM and NCM models for network
adapters. The SCSI commands a stick understands come from Seagate's public
command reference, because the committee drafts that define them aren't
public. The overall shape is Linux's: USB lives in the kernel, drivers bind to
the parts of a device through tables of IDs, the controller is taken from the
firmware the way Linux's code does it, and USB disks get Linux's `sd` names.
Redox's MIT-licensed USB programs (`xhcid`, `usbhidd`, `usbscsid`), which run
in user space, were read as an influence. What is ours is the split: every
format and state machine lives in two crates that allocate nothing and are
tested on the host against a simulated controller.

The rest of the page explains what a USB controller does, how SlopOS finds and
names the devices behind it, how each one reaches a driver, what the three
kinds of driver do, and what happens when a device leaves.

## What a USB controller does

USB is a tree. At the root is the computer's *host controller*, with a row of
ports. A *hub* plugged into a port adds more ports, a hub can sit behind a
hub, and devices sit at the leaves. The controller does the work on the
wire: the timing, the packets, the retries. The kernel only tells it what to
send and receive, and it does that through memory the controller reads and
writes by itself (DMA):

- a *command ring*, where the kernel leaves orders for the controller itself
  ("give the device on port 3 an address");
- a *transfer ring* for each channel to each device, where the kernel leaves
  the data to send or the space to receive into;
- an *event ring*, where the controller reports each command or transfer it
  finished, and raises an interrupt;
- a *doorbell* register, which the kernel writes to say "look at your rings".

Each of those channels is an *endpoint*: a numbered, one-way pipe on a
device. Every device has endpoint 0, which carries *control requests* ("what
are you?", "use configuration 1"). A stick adds two *bulk* endpoints, one in
and one out, for its data; a keyboard adds an *interrupt* endpoint that
reports which keys are down.

xHCI is the standard interface for USB 3 controllers, and one xHCI controller
serves devices of every USB speed. It is the only kind SlopOS drives, which
covers QEMU's recommended controller and both controllers of the
[tested laptop](/docs/status/hardware-requirements#the-tested-laptop). The
older EHCI, OHCI and UHCI controllers have no driver.

### Taking a controller from the firmware

The firmware uses USB before the operating system starts, so you can type in
its menus, and on some machines it keeps driving the controller behind the
system's back. xHCI defines a handshake for taking it over. SlopOS sets the
*OS owned* flag, waits up to a second (the limit the specification gives) for
the firmware to clear its *BIOS owned* flag, and clears it itself if the
firmware never does. Then it turns off the interrupts the firmware uses to
run its own code when the controller does something (*SMIs*), because some
firmware reports a clean handover and leaves them armed.

After that the controller is halted, stopped from reaching memory, reset,
given its rings and started. As a last check SlopOS sends it a command that
does nothing and waits for the answer: a controller whose rings or interrupt
don't work shows up here, at boot, rather than later as a device that never
appears. The log says which way it went:

```text
USB: xhci 1 taken from the firmware
USB: xhci 1 running on MSI
```

A controller SlopOS can't drive is left to the firmware, untouched apart from
its power state. That is one without message-based interrupts (MSI-X or MSI),
one that can only reach the first 4 GiB of memory, one that asks for more
than 512 pages of scratch memory or can't use 4 KiB pages, and any past the
eighth. The `usb=` [boot option](/docs/abi/boot-options) takes three values: `on`,
the default; `off`, which leaves every controller to the firmware; and
`report`, which logs each controller's capabilities and ports and then leaves
it to the firmware, a safe first look at an unfamiliar machine.

## How devices are found and named

When a port says something was connected, the kernel *enumerates* the
device, one step at a time:

1. It waits for the connection to stop bouncing, then resets the port if its
   kind of port needs that.
2. It asks the controller for a *slot*, the controller's record of one
   device, and has the controller give the device an address on the bus.
3. It reads the device's *descriptors*, what the device says about itself:
   its vendor and product numbers, its class, and its *configurations*, each
   a list of interfaces and their endpoints.
4. It picks a configuration, tells the controller about its endpoints, and
   offers the device's parts to the drivers.

Only one device per controller is ever between steps 1 and 2, because until
it has an address every new device answers to the same one. Every wait is
bounded by the timings USB 2.0 gives, and a control request has five seconds.
A device that fails three times has its port disabled until it is
unplugged, and the log says `USB: 1-3 disabled until unplugged`.

Hubs belong to the kernel's USB core, not to a driver. A hub is configured
like any device, and then its ports are watched and its devices enumerated
just like the controller's own. A slow device (a keyboard, say) behind a fast
hub talks through the hub's *transaction translator*, which the kernel names
to the controller for each such device. Hubs may be stacked five deep, as the
specification allows.

Controllers are numbered from 1 in the order they were found. `1-4` is root
port 4 of controller 1, and `1-4.2` is port 2 of the hub on that port. The
kernel log's USB lines name devices this way. The name says where a device
is, not what it is: the same stick in another port has another name, and its
disk takes the lowest free `sd` letter either way.

When a device offers several configurations, SlopOS picks the first one in
which a driver it has matches something and whose power the port can supply,
or else the first one the port can power. QEMU's USB
network adapter, for example, lists a configuration for the RNDIS protocol
first, which SlopOS doesn't speak, and a standard Ethernet one second.

## How a device reaches a driver

A device can do several things at once, like a keyboard with a separate
interface for its volume keys, or a network adapter with one interface for
status and another for data. Each part a driver serves is a *function*: an
interface, or several interfaces the device declares belong together. Each
function is offered to the registered USB drivers in priority order, exactly
as a PCI device is offered to PCI drivers ([Drivers](/docs/architecture/drivers)
explains the matching), and the first driver to accept it owns it. Once every
function has an answer, the log names the device and its drivers:

```text
USB: 1-4.1 attached, full speed
USB: 1-4.1 LUN 0 is sda, 1 MB in 512-byte blocks
USB: 1-4.1 46f4:0001 1 function, bound usb-storage
```

SlopOS has three USB drivers:

| Driver | Takes | Becomes |
|---|---|---|
| `usb-hid` | Keyboards, mice and tablets (class `03`) | Keys in the keyboard state, motion of the cursor |
| `usb-storage` | Sticks and other disks speaking Bulk-Only SCSI (class `08`, subclass `06`, protocol `50`) | Disks `sda`, `sdb`, ... |
| `usb-net` | Ethernet adapters speaking CDC ECM or NCM (class `02`, subclass `06` or `0D`) | Interfaces `eth1` and up |

A function no driver takes is listed as having `no driver` and left alone.

Two kernel threads serve every controller. The **`usb`** thread runs the
controllers: it notices ports changing, steps each enumeration, recovers
endpoints that stopped, repeats held keys and sets keyboard lights. It never
waits for a device to answer: it starts the next step and comes back when
the answer arrives, so a slow device delays only itself. It also runs at
least once a second, which catches a lost interrupt or a controller that
died. The **`usb-bind`** thread runs the drivers' probes and removals, which
may wait, for example while a stick spins up.

USB enumeration starts only once every PCI and ACPI device has been offered
its driver, so the machine's own disks and network cards get the first names
(`nvme0n1`, `eth0`) whatever is plugged in.

## Keyboards, mice and tablets

A HID device (*human interface device*: keyboards, mice, tablets, game pads)
describes the reports it sends in a *report descriptor*, a small table that
says "bits 0 to 7 are modifier keys, the next six bytes are key codes" or
"these two 16-bit fields are an X and a Y position". `usb-hid` reads every
device through its descriptor. There is also a fixed *boot protocol*, a
simple format a BIOS can read without parsing anything, and SlopOS uses it
only when a keyboard's or mouse's descriptor can't be read or describes
neither keys nor a pointer. Reading the descriptor first is deliberate: a
device starts in the descriptor's format, so the descriptor is right whether
or not the device honours a request to switch. At least one wireless
keyboard receiver acknowledges the switch to the boot protocol and keeps
sending its own format, which read as boot reports typed the wrong
letters.

Each report arrives on the device's interrupt endpoint, is decoded the moment
the controller reports it, and goes into the machine's one keyboard state or
one cursor, the same ones the built-in keyboard and touchpad feed. [Keyboards
and pointers](/docs/architecture/drivers#keyboards-and-pointers) explains how
several keyboards and mice share them. A few things are USB's own:

- A USB keyboard doesn't repeat a held key by itself, so the `usb` thread
  does: after half a second, about thirty times a second.
- Each keyboard is told when Caps Lock, Num Lock or Scroll Lock change, so its
  lights match the others'.
- Keys are read only from the parts of a descriptor that call themselves a
  keyboard or keypad, and motion only from a mouse or pointer, so a game pad
  moves nothing. Media keys (volume, play) aren't read.
- Unplugging a keyboard or mouse releases every key and button it held.

A USB keyboard is the physical console as much as the built-in one, so the
[diagnostic console](/docs/development/diagnostics)'s Alt+PrintScreen works on
it too.

## USB sticks

A stick speaks *SCSI*, the command set first written for server disks:
"read 240 blocks from block 2048", "how big are you?". Over USB those commands travel in the
*Bulk-Only Transport*: the kernel sends a 31-byte command wrapper on the bulk
out endpoint, the data moves on one of the two bulk endpoints, and the stick
answers with a 13-byte status wrapper on the bulk in endpoint.

A stick may hold several disks, called *logical units* (a card reader with
four slots has four). For each one, `usb-storage` asks what it is, waits for
it to become ready (for up to `usb.settle_ms`, five seconds by default),
reads its size and checks the write-protect switch. A unit that is a disk with
512-, 1024-, 2048- or 4096-byte blocks registers with the block layer, which
names it `sda`, `sdb` and so on, the lowest letter free. A card-reader slot
with no card is passed over. From there a stick is a disk like any other:
it has partitions, `/dev/disk/by-*` links and claims, as [Disks](/docs/architecture/storage)
describes. A stick whose switch is set to read-only reports so, and a mount of
it is read-only.

Each stick keeps one command on the wire and one queued behind it, and every
read or write is cut into pieces of at most 120 KiB. That is the limit Linux
keeps for USB disks slower than SuperSpeed, because some devices fail on
anything larger; SlopOS keeps it at every speed. Sticks
also fail in ways a built-in disk doesn't, so every command has a deadline
and a ladder of recovery behind it, each rung heavier than the last:

1. A stalled transfer is cleared, and the stick is asked for its status.
2. A command the stick reports as failed is followed by a request for the
   reason, which decides whether it is tried again.
3. A garbled status, a protocol error, or a command still unanswered after
   20 seconds gets a *reset recovery*: the Bulk-Only reset request, the
   stalls cleared on both sides, and the endpoints set up afresh. Then the
   command is sent once more.
4. If that fails, the port is reset and the stick is enumerated again, as if
   it had been plugged in anew. That uses up one of the port's three tries,
   unless the stick had been working for a minute.

### Pulling a stick

A stick can leave while it is mounted, and while a program is writing to it.
When it does, its disk is unregistered and every request still waiting fails
at once instead of timing out. The filesystem on it turns read-only, as ext4
does on a disk that fails: it refuses every change with `EROFS`, fails every
read that would need the stick, and stays mounted until you unmount it.
Nothing unmounts it by force. What a program saved with `fsync` before the
pull is on the stick, as [Crash recovery](/docs/architecture/crash-recovery)
promises for any crash. The log records the departure:

```text
USB: 1-4.1 detached
USB: 1-4.1 sda removed
USB: 1-4.1 removed from slot 3
```

At poweroff and reboot each controller's shutdown hook drains its sticks'
queues, tells every stick that has a write cache to write it out, and only
then resets the controller. A stick written raw through `/dev/sda` is flushed
the same way.

## Network adapters

Most USB Ethernet adapters offer a standard configuration besides the
vendor's own: CDC *ECM*, which carries one Ethernet frame per transfer, or
*NCM*, which packs several frames into one transfer. `usb-net` speaks both.
It reads the adapter's MAC address from the device, declines one that can't
carry a full 1500-byte packet or names a MAC that isn't a valid unicast
address, keeps four receive transfers posted, and publishes the adapter to
the network stack like any network card:

```text
USB: 1-4.4 is eth1, 40:54:00:12:34:99, ECM
```

The adapter takes the lowest free `ethN` name, so the machine's own card
keeps `eth0`. It also gets a DHCP client without anyone asking, which is why
the stack is careful about what a lease from a device someone just plugged in
may change: [Networking](/docs/architecture/networking) explains the rules.
When the adapter leaves, its interface, routes and DHCP client go with it
and the name is free again. Whether the cable is plugged in is what the
adapter last reported; an adapter that never reports it is taken as always
connected.

## When the boot waits for USB

The boot doesn't wait for USB to finish finding devices, except where
something on the command line depends on it:

- when `root=` or a `mount=` names a device that isn't there yet, the boot
  waits up to `usb.settle_ms` for USB to settle, in case it is a stick;
- when the live system looks for the toolchain partition on the stick it was
  started from ([Installing SlopOS](/docs/self-hosting/installing));
- under `tests=on`, where the `usb settle` step waits up to a minute and
  fails the run with `USB: unsettled`, so every test run sees the same bus.

USB has *settled* once every port has been powered long enough for a device
to announce itself and has been quiet since, and every device that connected
has reached an end: bound, declined, matched by no driver or given up on,
with every new disk's partition table read. The USB threads run on the other
processors while the boot waits, so on a machine with a single processor the
wait is skipped and the log says so.

`root=auto` and `root=disk` never choose a USB disk: they mean the machine's
own disk, and a stick left in a port must not change what boots. A root on a
stick can be named with `root=PARTUUID=...`, which waits for it, but no test
boots one.

## What it means in practice

- **Mounting a stick takes the `Mount` capability**, as mounting any disk
  does, so at a shell you ask for it with
  [`priv`](/docs/architecture/privd), which asks before it runs anything:

  ```bash
  mkdir ~/stick
  priv mount /dev/sda1 ~/stick
  priv umount ~/stick
  ```

  `mount` takes a device as ext4 unless `-t` says otherwise. The kernel
  mounts only ext2, ext3 and ext4, so a FAT stick gets its device node and
  its `/dev/disk/by-label` link but can't be mounted.
- **Unmount before you pull.** Pulling a mounted stick loses what nobody
  synced and leaves the mount read-only until you unmount it.
- **To see the bus**, run `kconsole u`. It lists every controller, port,
  device, function, driver and endpoint queue, in lines like these (the
  slot numbers vary):

  ```text
  usb:   1-4 slot 2 0409:55aa full speed, hub
  usb:   1-4.1 slot 3 46f4:0001 full speed, configured
  usb:     function 0 interface 0 class 08/06/50: bound usb-storage
  usb:     ep 0x81 dci 3 queued 0
  ```

  `kconsole` asks privd for the `SysInspect` capability it needs, which
  privd grants an administrator without asking. The same listing is the
  diagnostic console's `u` key.
- **On a machine you don't know yet**, boot once with `usb=report` to see
  its controllers without taking them from the firmware.

## What USB devices SlopOS doesn't support

- Controllers other than xHCI (EHCI, OHCI, UHCI), and controllers that come
  and go: docks, USB4 and Thunderbolt tunnels, Type-C alternate modes.
- Isochronous transfers, so no audio devices or cameras.
- Bluetooth, USB serial adapters, printers, smart cards, phones (MTP), and
  other kinds of device.
- RNDIS network adapters, by decision: the protocol is insecure by design
  against a hostile device.
- UAS, the faster protocol for USB 3 disks; a UAS disk is used through the
  Bulk-Only setting it also offers.
- Power management: suspend, and the low-power link states.
- Media change in card readers: the disk is the card that was present when
  the reader was plugged in.
- USB on the panic path: a panic's record is written to the NVMe disk, and
  the panic screen reads only the built-in keyboard, so a machine whose only
  keyboard is USB should boot with `panic=reboot`.

## Differences from Linux

| | Linux | SlopOS |
|---|---|---|
| What a driver binds to | An interface | A function: an interface, or the interfaces declared to belong together |
| Access from programs | `usbfs` at `/dev/bus/usb/`, and user-space drivers on libusb | None. A device is reachable only as its disk, interface or input |
| Largest request to a stick | 120 KiB, or 1 MiB at SuperSpeed, with a quirk list for devices that need less | 120 KiB at every speed, with no quirk list yet |
| Network adapters | ECM, NCM and RNDIS | ECM and NCM |
| Keyboards and mice | Each device separate for programs; merged for the text console | Merged once, in the kernel, for every consumer |

## How we know it works

Every parser of something a device sends (descriptors, reports, status
wrappers, network transfer blocks, strings) runs on the host under deliberate
corruption, and must neither panic nor read past its input. The whole
enumeration and recovery logic runs on the host against a simulated
controller with simulated hubs, keyboards and sticks. That simulation is
there because QEMU's controller lacks things real ones have: QEMU never
makes the firmware hand a controller over, has only a full-speed hub, and
its sticks keep no *data toggles* (an alternating bit each end keeps to spot
a lost or repeated packet), which a recovery that resets only one end gets
wrong.

`just test` attaches one QEMU controller with a scratch stick, `sda`. `just
test-usb` boots a kernel with both of QEMU's controller models, one on MSI-X
and one on MSI, each carrying a stick at every speed, a hub with devices
behind it and a keyboard, plus an ext4 stick, a read-only drive and a
network adapter. The host then plugs and pulls devices through QEMU's
control socket and types and moves the mouse through it, and the run passes
only if every device enumerates, binds and leaves cleanly twice over with
nothing left behind; held keys repeat, Caps Lock lights every keyboard and
SysRq runs a command; every mouse and the tablet move the one cursor; a
stick pulled under a program that keeps calling `fsync` turns its mount
read-only and comes back holding what was synced; the network adapter takes
a lease and carries TCP; a shell typed at on the USB keyboard runs what was
typed; and each controller is reset at the end. On real hardware, only the
tested laptop's two controllers, with a USB mouse and keyboard, have been
tried ([Hardware requirements](/docs/status/hardware-requirements#the-tested-laptop)).

## For contributors

- **Formats are data.** xHCI's registers, rings and contexts, descriptors,
  hubs, Bulk-Only and SCSI, and CDC live in `usb-core`; HID lives in
  `hid-core`, which the I²C touchpad shares. Both allocate nothing and are
  tested by `just test-host`. A parser of device input bounds every read and
  joins the mutation loops.
- **The event ring is the only completion path.** The interrupt handler
  drains it into per-endpoint queues, allocating nothing, then wakes the
  block engine, netpoll or the USB threads. A `ReportSink` or
  `TransferSink` is called right there and may not block, allocate or log.
- **Nothing slow under a spinlock.** No bring-up, port reset, enumeration or
  control transfer runs under one, and a hub's lock is never held while a
  child's is taken. `QueueOps` and `NetDevice::tx` run with interrupts off.
- **A command that never completes kills the controller.** Commands run in
  ring order, so one stuck command holds every later one: after five seconds
  the controller is halted, taken off the bus and every device on it removed
  without a command.
- **Removal is ordered.** The device is marked gone, its endpoints stopped,
  its transfers failed, its claims released and their removals run on the
  bind thread, then its slot disabled and its pages freed. A driver whose
  objects outlive the binding (a stick's disk does, until its last mount
  goes) must touch no ring or page of a device that is gone.
- **A halt is cleared on both sides.** Resetting the endpoint resets the
  controller's data toggle and `CLEAR_FEATURE(ENDPOINT_HALT)` resets the
  device's; a driver that owns its pipes through `BoundDevice::stream`
  recovers them itself, as Bulk-Only must.
- **Rings and contexts are single zeroed pages**, so nothing crosses a page
  or a 64 KiB boundary. A ring entry (TRB) is written with integer stores,
  its cycle bit last, behind a fence, since that bit hands it to the
  controller.
- **The specifications are the source.** No code or prose comes from Linux or
  QEMU, and spec prose stays out of comments, which cite sections.
  [Add a driver](/docs/development/adding-a-driver#a-usb-driver) shows the
  handles a USB driver gets.
