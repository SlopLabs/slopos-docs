The SlopOS desktop is meant to read as one product: the window frames, the
dock, the lock screen, the installer and every app use the same colours, the
same type, the same spacing and the same words. You get that without trying
when you build an app from **appkit**, SlopOS's toolkit, because the toolkit's
components carry the look and the behaviour: text takes a role rather than a
size, and a gate refuses an app that makes a colour of its own. What it
doesn't promise: there is one dark theme, one screen scale and no way for a
person to choose another yet.

This page describes the theme, the components, the checks that hold apps to
them, the parts of the desktop the compositor draws, and the apps that ship.
How pixels and input travel between apps and the compositor is on
[Windowing](/docs/architecture/userland-windowing); writing your own app is
[Write an app](/docs/development/writing-an-app).

## Where it comes from

None of the ideas here are new. Colours are named by the surface they sit on
and by what they mean, as libadwaita, GNOME's toolkit, names its own (window,
view and card colours; accent, destructive, success, warning and error). The
contrast rules are WCAG 2.2's: 4.5:1 for text, 3:1 for the edges of controls
and focus indicators. Icons are glyphs of Lucide, an open icon set shipped as
a font. App icons follow GNOME's and Apple's guidance: a few simple filled
shapes, no shadows of their own. An app is a function from its state to a
view plus a function that applies messages, which is the Elm architecture.
And refusing a raw colour where a named one belongs is what the
[`HardCodedColor`](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/prebuilts/checkcolor/+/refs/tags/android-12.0.0_r14/README.md)
lint check does for parts of Android's own source, which
must take their colours from theme attributes; here the check is one of the
gates every change must pass.

## Why the look is enforced rather than suggested

Before the theme existed, each app picked its own colours and sizes. Page
titles came out the same size as the paragraph under them, a disabled option
dimmed its title but not its reason, and one app had three names: *Editor* in
the dock, *Sloped* in its title bar and "SlopOS editor" in its About box.
Nothing about this is hard to fix once found, but finding it means looking at
every screen at every width after every change, which nobody does. So SlopOS
moves the decisions into code (a theme of named roles and components that
apply them), makes the shortcuts fail a gate, and checks the result by
laying every screen out off screen and measuring it.

## The theme

`theme-core` holds the desktop's design values, and both appkit and the
compositor read them through it. No app built on appkit defines a colour of
its own, except where colours are its content, such as the editor's syntax
palette.

- **Colours are roles.** A surface (`Base` for a window's content, `Raised`
  for cards, popovers and dialogs, `Sunken` for fields and lists, `Chrome`
  for title and menu bars), a text *tone* (`Normal`, `Secondary`,
  `Disabled`, `Accent`, `Danger`, `Warning`, `Success`, `Info`), and the
  edges, focus ring and selection. Every status hue exists twice: a light
  *ink* for text on a surface, and a deep *fill* for white text on it, which
  is how a filled button or a danger button is drawn. Hover and pressed are
  derived from the role, never listed per control: a filled control lightens
  under the pointer and darkens while pressed. The fills are roles of their
  own (`AccentFill`, `DangerFill` and so on), and `Tone::fill` gives the fill
  that goes with a tone. Every control is filled (a secondary button in the
  control colour, a field sunken inside a quiet edge), so none reads as an
  outline.
- **Text has roles too.** Each role is a size, a weight and a line height:

  | Role | Size and weight | Used for |
  |---|---|---|
  | `Hero` | 64 px semibold | the time on the lock and login screens, nothing else |
  | `Display` | 24 px semibold | a page that is one number |
  | `Title` | 18 px semibold | a page's title |
  | `Heading` | 15 px semibold | a section, the menu bar's app name |
  | `Body` | 14 px | text |
  | `Label` | 13 px | controls, the dock's labels |
  | `Caption` | 12 px | small print under something |
  | `Mono` | 13 px | code and command lines |

- **Space comes in steps** of 4 px (`space::XS` 4, `SM` 8, `MD` 12, `LG` 16,
  `XL` 24), with named insets for a page, a section, a card and a control.
  Controls have 6 px corners, cards and windows 12 px. Focus is drawn 2 px
  wide where an edge is 1 px, so it shows by more than a change of colour.
- **The chrome's own values** live there as well: the wallpaper's gradient,
  the glass of the dock and the menu bar, the rims and shadows of windows,
  eight app tile hues and seven distinct series colours for charts.

There is one table, a dark graphite one. A light theme would be a second
table, with no change to any app.

Code reaches the theme in one of two ways. appkit hands every widget the
theme through its style sheet (`StyleSheet::theme`), so an app only names
what it wants: a `Tone`, a `Surface`, a `TextRole` for any text, or
`SegmentFill::Series` for one segment of a chart. Code that draws itself, as
the compositor's frames and dock do, reads the table with `theme::current()`.

Interface text is set in Inter, code and the terminal in JetBrains Mono, and
icons in Lucide, all loaded at run time from `/usr/share/fonts`. They are
never compiled into a program, because the text fonts' licence (the SIL Open
Font License) and SlopOS's own could not both govern one binary. Every glyph
in a line sits on one baseline, pairs are kerned from the font's own table,
and measuring, fitting and drawing share one layout, so text that measured
as fitting draws as fitting.

## Components

An app describes its window as a tree of *components*, each of which says
what a thing is rather than how to draw it: a `Page` (a title, a sentence about
it, content that scrolls and a row of buttons under a separator), a
`Section`, a `PropertyList` of names and values, a `Notice`, a `ButtonRow`, a
`List` of `ListRow`s, a `Sidebar`, a `Toolbar` of `IconButton`s, a
`SearchField`, a `StatusBar`, a `Tag`, an `EmptyState`, a `Disclosure`, a
`Dialog`, a `Graph`, and `Text` in a role and a tone. For lists of many
things and documents there are tables, trees, a code view, tabs, menus and a
zoomable picture. A page of settings is a `Group`, a box of rows with a
hairline between each: a `Switch` for what turns on and off, a `PropertyList`
of how things stand now, and `ActionRow`s, each ending in the control that
changes what the row names, such as *Edit…*. The appkit guide in the source
tree, `appkit/GUIDE.md`, says which to use when.

What makes them worth using is what comes with every one of them:

- **Text that doesn't fit is cut with `…`**, at its end or, for a path or a
  file name, at its start, and the whole text shows in a tooltip. Text set to
  wrap breaks at spaces and shows every word, unless the app caps its number
  of lines, when the last line is cut with `…` too.
- **Every control has its states.** It lights under the pointer, shows when
  pressed, can be disabled and shows that too. Focus is one ring around the
  control, or around the row the keyboard is on in a list, table, tree or
  menu. A field thickens its own edge in the focus colour instead. A click
  gives focus to a field, a list or a table but leaves it where it was for a
  button, so a toolbar button never takes the keyboard from a search field.
- **The keyboard works the same everywhere.** Tab and Shift+Tab move focus.
  Enter and Escape that no control took press the confirming and the back or
  cancel button of the page or dialog in front, and Enter never reaches a
  destructive button that way: only one that has focus takes it. A dialog
  keeps the keyboard and the pointer until it is answered or dismissed. It
  opens with the keyboard in its first field, so what you type goes there at
  once, and gives focus back to where it was when it closes. Left and Right
  move along a row of buttons. A key held down types and moves again as it
  repeats, but presses the focused control only once.
- **Every control and every row is at least 28 px across**, so a pointer can
  find it.
- **A change the system makes shows at once.** A `Switch` acts the moment it
  is turned, with no button after it. While the system is still making the
  change the switch is *pending*, never disabled: its knob is where you
  turned it, its track still shows what the system does, and it stays a
  control you can press again. GTK's switch separates the same two things
  as `active` and `state`. An app keeps what it asked for in an
  `appkit::Pending` and builds each control from the value that says to
  show, so a second press while the first is pending is one more change
  asked, and the answers come back in the order asked.
- **A rebuild keeps your place.** The app rebuilds its whole view after
  every change, yet a scroll view and a table stay scrolled where they were
  and a menu keeps the row the arrows reached, as long as the new view shows
  the same thing. A page counts as the same while its title is the same, so
  a notice that comes and goes moves nothing you scrolled to.
- **Scrolling goes where it can.** The wheel moves the innermost view under
  the pointer that can still move, the rows of a table or a tree lie beside
  the scrollbar rather than under it, and a scroll view is a stop for Tab
  only while it has something to scroll.
- **Windows describe themselves.** An app states the size its window opens
  at, the least it can be laid out at, and whether it resizes, and the
  compositor holds it to that. A window showing a document is titled
  `<document> — <App>`, any other by the app's name alone, and that name is
  the one its dock entry uses.
- **Refusals say why.** When the computer refuses to open or change a file,
  appkit works out the reason from what the kernel says about the file and
  the filesystem it is on (`statfs` and the file's flags, never its path):
  the system's own file, an extension's, one locked against changes, a disk
  that can't be written, or permissions. The app says which.

The widget gallery (`widget_gallery`, or `widget_gallery --page <name>` for
one of its pages) shows every component in each of its states.

## How the look is checked

Three kinds of check hold apps to all this, none of which needs anyone to look
at a screen.

**The theme's own tests.** `theme-core`'s host tests compute WCAG 2.2's
contrast ratio for the pairs the desktop puts together: body and
secondary text against every surface, hovered and selected rows included, at
4.5:1; status inks on plain surfaces at 4.5:1; white text on every fill in
every state at 4.5:1; and edges, the focus ring, disabled text and an app
icon's glyph against its tile at 3:1. Disabled text is held to 3:1 although
WCAG asks nothing of inactive controls. Another test checks that every icon
the code names is a glyph of the shipped font.

**The layout audit.** `appkit::audit` lays a tree out at a given size, paints
it off screen and measures what came out. It reports text cut by an edge
instead of an ellipsis, siblings that overlap, a child outside its parent,
text over text, a row whose texts don't share a baseline, a control whose
focus doesn't show all the way round, a control under 28 px, and text under
its contrast floor against the colour actually behind it. `appkit_test` holds
every component and every gallery page to it at 360, 640 and 960 px wide,
after first planting each kind of mistake to see that the audit finds it. The
consent dialog, the installer's pages, the System Monitor and the compositor's
own screens are audited the same way.

One view passing the audit says nothing about what happens between two. So
the audit also compares views: `audit::audit_transition` takes a page before
and after a control was used and reports what the change costs whoever just
used it (the control gone or somewhere else, or the keyboard no longer on
it), and `audit::audit_moved` reports every control both views show that
moved. A page that asks the system for a change holds each step, from the
press to the answer, to them in a test.

**Gates on the source.** The `ui-literals` gate (`xtask lint ui-literals`,
which `just check-framekernel-gates` runs) fails an app that constructs or
derives a colour, or gives a spacing or an inset a number. An app here is
any file under `apps/`, a Settings panel in `settings-app/`, or a module of
`userland/src/apps/` that implements `App` or calls `run_app`; appkit itself
is held to constructing no colour. A file whose colours are its content,
such as the editor's syntax palette, is exempt from the colour check by a
line in `scripts/gates/ui-literals.txt` that says why, and a line that
exempts nothing fails the gate. The `app-deps` gate (`xtask lint app-deps`)
fails a crate under `apps/` that depends on anything but appkit, the
editor's core and the image decoder, so no app reaches past the toolkit into
the kernel's interface or the toolkit's internals.

## Words

Words are checked too, wherever a screen keeps them as data (a menu's
entries, privd's policy, the installer's pages) rather than spelling them out
in drawing code. The glossary in `theme_core::words` lists what a screen a
person acts on never says, and what it says instead:

| Never | Instead |
|---|---|
| machine | computer |
| drive | disk |
| medium | installer |
| volume, region | partition |
| reboot | restart |
| halt, shut down | power off |
| admin | administrator |
| end session | log out |
| switch person | switch user |
| program, client | app |
| boot order, firmware entry | startup order |

A *label* (a button, a menu entry, a heading) starts with a capital and has no
full stop; a *sentence* starts with a capital and ends with a full stop or a
question mark; neither names a command-line flag or writes an ellipsis as
three dots. Buttons are verbs, and an ellipsis marks one that asks something
further (*Restart…*). Everything uses sentence case, unlike GNOME's
guidelines, which capitalise headings and buttons. Memory and file sizes are
binary (`512 bytes`, `1.5 KiB`, `8 MiB`), and disk sizes decimal, as a
drive's label gives them. Host tests hold privd's policy (its words are
`priv-core::words`), every page of the installer and the compositor's menus
and screens to these rules.

## What the compositor draws

The compositor draws everything on screen that isn't an app's content.

<DesktopAnatomy />

- **The desktop** is the theme's wallpaper, a dark gradient lit softly from
  above, drawn at the screen's size and dithered so it shows no bands. The
  theme has several such gradients, its own graphite one first, and a
  person's preferences choose one and, optionally, a picture to lay over it,
  filled, fitted, centred, stretched or tiled
  ([Settings and preferences](/docs/architecture/settings)). The picture is
  decoded on a thread of its own, and the last one stays on screen until the
  new one is ready. The login screen is nobody's, so it shows the defaults.
- **Window frames** are rounded at all four corners, with a dark hairline
  just outside and a light one along the top, and a shadow that is half as
  dark on a window without focus; a maximised window is square and has
  neither hairlines nor shadow. The close, minimise and zoom buttons are
  coloured discs that show their glyphs while the pointer is over the three
  of them, and are grey on a window without focus until the pointer comes
  over them; a window that can't be resized has a grey zoom button with no
  glyph. A resize takes a press up to 8 px outside the frame. Zoom fills the
  space between the menu bar and the dock, or gives the window the nearest
  size its limits allow, centred; pressing it again puts the window back, as
  long as it is still maximised or still where zoom put it.
- **A new window** opens centred between the menu bar and the dock when it
  is its app's first. A later one of the same app opens down and to the
  right of the app's latest by the height of a title bar, and goes back to
  the top of the area before its frame would cross the dock. A window that
  asks for more room than there is gets the whole area, or its own least
  size if that is larger.
- **The menu bar** is a strip of glass across the top. On the left,
  SlopOS's mark opens the system menu and the focused window's app is named;
  on the right, the network item opens the network panel and the clock shows
  the time as `HH:MM`, or as `2:05 PM` when the person's preferences ask for
  a twelve-hour clock, in UTC, since SlopOS has no time zones, with the date
  a click away. The app is named by its dock entry, or by the window's title
  when no entry has its app id.
- **The system menu** names the person and offers *Lock screen*, *Switch
  user…*, *Log out…*, *Restart…* and *Power off…*. Only once a new base has
  changed the desktop's own programs does *Restart desktop…* appear, at the
  top, under the notice *Restart the desktop to finish updating*, and the
  menu then opens by itself to offer it. Each entry that ends something asks
  first, with *Cancel* focused, so Enter alone never ends anything. Logging
  out and restarting the desktop are init's to carry out; restarting and
  powering off the computer are privd's (`org.slopos.power.reboot` and
  `.poweroff`). When one of them doesn't happen, the question's place says
  so, unless you declined privd's own question, and the reason goes to the
  log.
- **The dock** floats as a pane of glass above the bottom of the screen. It
  holds the pinned apps, then the running apps that no entry names, a dot
  under each app that runs, and each icon grows a little as the pointer
  nears and is named in a label above it while the pointer is over it.
- **The lock and login screens** dim the wallpaper and show the time and
  date, the person's initial in a circle and their name (or, on the login
  screen, the people as rows, five at a time), a password field and *Unlock*
  (beside *Switch user…*) or *Log in*. Either screen comes up with the
  password field focused, so the person chosen types their password at once,
  as LightDM's and SDDM's greeters do. On the login screen the wheel moves
  the choice of person; so do the arrows, and typing, once Tab has reached
  the list, and typing chooses the first person whose name starts with what
  was typed. What the screen says back, such as a wrong password, sits on a
  notice. Once a password has passed the login screen takes nothing more,
  and it starts over each time it comes in front, or after half a minute if
  the desktop it was starting never does.
- **Questions** are drawn over everything: the ones the system menu asks
  before it ends something, and privd's consent dialog. The consent dialog is
  a card centred over the window of the app that asks, when it has one, with
  the headline `<App> wants to <do something>.`, the app's icon, what
  follows from allowing it, *Cancel*, and a button naming what it allows,
  filled with the danger tone when the action destroys something.
  [Privileged actions](/docs/architecture/privd) explains how it guards what
  you type and click.

The frames and the dock are drawn by the compositor's own drawing code, but the
compositor's other surfaces (the lock and login screens, the system menu, the
panels and the questions) are appkit trees like any app's, laid out and
painted into the compositor's own buffer (`appkit::embed`), so they are built
from the same components and audited the same way.

They differ from an app in who decides what a key does. `appkit::embed`
takes the pointer, but on the lock and login screens, in the system menu and
in a question every key goes to a small model that knows what has focus
(`chrome-core::gate`, `::menu` and `::key`, with the trees that show them in
`compositor::views`). Those models are plain code with no screen, so their
tests run on the build machine, and Tab, the arrows, Space and Enter work in
them as in any form. The network and clock panels answer the pointer alone
and take a key only to close on Escape. While the lock or login screen or a
question is up no window hears the pointer, and a button's release goes only
to the surface its press reached.

[Sessions and login](/docs/architecture/sessions) covers what locking,
switching user and logging out do.

## App icons

Every app icon is one white glyph on a rounded tile in one of the theme's
tile hues, lit slightly from its top-left corner and nothing more: no sheen,
no rim, no shadow, since the dock casts the shadow. The shipped icons are
drawn by code (`just icons`, `tools/icons/src/art.rs`) from a few filled
shapes on a 64-unit grid. Each glyph fills a guide rectangle (a *keyline*:
landscape, portrait or square, all of about the same area), so no icon looks
bigger than another, and depth comes only from a shape behind at 60% white
or from detail cut out of a white shape.
They ship at 64 px and as `<id>@2x.png` at 128 px, and a host test fails when
a shipped PNG is not what the code draws. An app that ships no icon is shown
as a glyph of the icon font on a tile whose hue its app id picks
(`AppTile::of`, which never picks graphite: that hue is kept for tools).
`appkit::app_icon` draws the tile for both, so a shipped icon and a
stand-in look alike.

## How the dock uses app entries

The dock learns which apps exist from *app entries*, small files that name an
app, the program it runs and its icon. It reads them once, when the desktop
starts, so an app installed while you are logged in appears only once your
desktop starts again (after you log out and back in, for example). It
sorts them by name and shows at most 16 apps, pinned and running together. A
window belongs to an entry when its app id is the entry's id, which is also
how the menu bar names the focused window's app.

A click on an app that has a window brings that window to the front,
restoring it if it was minimised. A click on an app that isn't running starts
the program its entry names, with the desktop's environment. A window whose
app has no entry still gets an icon after the pinned ones, named by the
window's title.

[Installed software](/docs/architecture/software#app-entries) describes the
entry files, the places the dock reads them from and which entry wins when
two share an id; [Extensions](/docs/architecture/extensions) says what an
extension's entries may do.

## The apps that ship

Some apps are part of the base, the read-only system every boot runs; the
rest are installed at `/usr/local`, outside the base, so they can be updated
on their own ([Installed software](/docs/architecture/software) explains
why).

| App | Program | Where | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| Terminal | `terminal` | base | A shell on a *pseudo-terminal* (a pair of endpoints that look like a serial line to the shell), emulating an xterm closely enough for full-screen programs and mouse reporting. Resizing re-wraps long lines in the scrollback; Ctrl+Shift+PgUp and PgDn scroll it. |
| System Monitor | `sysmon` | base | A *Performance* tab with a graph of the last minute for the processor, memory, each disk, each network adapter and the graphics adapter, and a *Processes* tab listing every process with its threads folded in, which can end one (through privd when it belongs to another person, and saying why when it can't). `sysmon --page cpu`, `memory`, `disk`, `network`, `gpu` or `processes` opens on a page. |
| Install SlopOS | `installer-ui` | base | The installer's window, shown only when the computer started from the install medium ([Installing SlopOS](/docs/self-hosting/installing)). |
| Editor | `editor` | `/usr/local` | A text editor with tabs, a file tree, syntax highlighting, find and replace and a command palette. It saves through a synced copy renamed over the file, so a failed save never leaves half a file, and asks before replacing a file that changed since it was opened. Its manual is `share/slopos/doc/editor.md`. |
| Files | `file_manager` | `/usr/local` | A file manager with places in a sidebar, a toolbar and a table of the folder. |
| Image Viewer | `image_viewer` | `/usr/local` | Shows a PNG fitted to the window, zoomed with the wheel or `+` and `-`, `f` to fit again and `0` for actual size. |
| Widget Gallery | `widget_gallery` | `/usr/local` | Every appkit component in each of its states. It has no dock entry; start it from a terminal. |

## What the System Monitor measures

The System Monitor (`/bin/sysmon`, `apps::sysmon` in the source) samples the
kernel once a second and keeps the last minute of each figure. Its
*Performance* tab is a wide sidebar of resources, each led by a thumbnail of
its own graph, beside the chosen one's page of graphs, figures and details.
Its *Processes* tab names each process by its first thread's id, which is
what `getpid` returns (the kernel's own process ids number a different
space), and filters by name, user or id.
`sysmon --page cpu|memory|disk|network|gpu|processes` opens on one page,
which is how `just ui-shots` photographs each.

A share, such as processor load, is graphed from 0 to 100%, so its height
means the same from one minute to the next. A rate, such as bytes a second
to a disk, has no top of its own, so the graph's top is `nice_ceiling` of
the highest sample: a round value in steps of 1, 2 and 5, never under a
floor that keeps an idle graph flat.

The figures come from counters the kernel keeps and three calls any program
may make, none of which needs privilege:

| Call | What it reports |
|---|---|
| `process_memory` | For each live process, once: the user pages its address space holds now (`VmSpace::resident_pages`) and the most it has held |
| `block_stats` | For each whole disk, in the order the disks were registered: name, capacity, logical block size, transport (NVMe, virtio or USB), read-only and removable flags and, since it was registered, the bytes and requests served each way, the flushes sent and the busy time |
| `display_stats` | What drives the screen (the kernel's `ScanoutId`), its geometry and size and, since boot, the frames the adapter took from the compositor, the bytes copied to the screen, the time spent copying and the time spent in the adapter's present hook |

Busy time is what Linux calls `io_ticks`: wall time with at least one of the
disk's requests in flight, an asynchronous write counting until it
completes. Frames the kernel suppressed are not counted as taken.

## Looking at it

`just ui-shots` boots the live ISO under QEMU, plays scenes of keys, clicks
and commands typed at the serial console, and saves a PNG of each screen
under `builddir/ui-shots/`: each installer page, the consent dialog, the
system menu, the question *Power off…* asks, the clock's date, the lock and
login screens, the desktop with two windows and the pointer on the dock, each
app and each page of the gallery. Naming scenes takes only those (`just
ui-shots apps`; the scenes are `installer`, `chrome`, `desktop`, `apps` and
`gallery`), and `--no-install` stops the installer at its review page.
Nothing compares the shots with anything: they are for people to look at
before and after a change. The audit and the tests are the gate.

## For contributors

- **Name roles, never values.** An app names a `Tone`, a `Surface`, a
  `TextRole`, a `space` step or an `inset`, and appkit itself constructs no
  colour (the checks behind a picture's transparent pixels aside). A new role
  goes in `theme-core`, with a contrast test for every surface it may sit on.
- **A new component owns its behaviour.** Its states, focus ring, overflow and
  hit target are the component's job, and it joins the gallery
  (`appkit::catalogue`), so `appkit_test` audits it at three widths.
- **Words are data where they can be.** A surface whose words are data holds
  them to `theme_core::words` in a host test, and keeps trade words (device
  names, paths, sizes in bytes) in its details.
- **A new icon is a glyph of the shipped font.** Add an `Icon` variant naming
  Lucide's codepoint; the font test fails on one the font lacks. An app icon
  is a new `Art` in `tools/icons`, rendered with `just icons`.
- **A control is at least `style::MIN_TARGET` across**, and so is every row
  of a list, table, tree or menu. A pane's divider is the one exception: it
  is a divider, not a control. Sizes an app must count with come from
  appkit, never from a number of its own.
- **Enter and Escape have one route.** One that no control took goes to the
  `ButtonRow` ending the modal layer's `Page` or dialog (its confirming
  button, never a destructive one, and its back or cancel one) before the
  app's `on_key`. A `Page` opened from another takes `Page::back`, an arrow
  before its title which Escape presses ahead of the footer, and the row
  that opened it ends in an arrow (`ListRow::opens`). A table's chosen row
  opens on Enter or a second click (`on_activate`). A list or a table
  leaves an Alt chord to the app.
- **A layer over the page says what it covers.** `Widget::covers` stops the
  pointer and tooltips reaching what is beneath, `Widget::is_modal` stops
  Tab as well, and an event the layer stopped comes back as
  `EventResponse::Blocked`, not as unhandled, so the app never acts on a key
  meant for a dialog.
- **Focus is kept by place, not by widget.** Every widget is new after a
  rebuild, so `FocusManager` remembers where in the Tab order the keyboard
  was. A focused control that is disabled for a moment keeps its place:
  nothing has focus meanwhile, the rebuild that enables it focuses it again,
  and Tab goes on from where it was, unless a press, Tab or another page
  came first. Don't work around a lost focus by disabling less; keep what
  was asked in an `appkit::Pending` and leave the control enabled.
- **What a widget keeps across a rebuild it declares.** A widget whose
  position the app does not keep (a scroll offset, a menu's keyboard row)
  returns it from `Widget::kept`, and appkit hands it to the widget built in
  its place when that shows the same.
- **The window protocol says nothing of key repeats.** `run_app` tells a
  repeat from a press by the keys it has seen go down and not up
  (`run::Held`). A new control gets "types again, presses once" from that,
  and must not track held keys itself.
- **Text that is cut says so.** A line too long ends, or for a path starts,
  with `…` (`ListRow::detail_tail` is the detail cut at its start) and shows
  whole in a tooltip. A view onto content that goes on past it, as a scroll
  view or a field is, paints through `PaintContext::with_viewport`, which
  tells the audit that what its edge cuts is not cut short.
- **An app reads its window once and its title often.** `App::window` (a
  `WindowSpec`) is read when the app starts and sent to the compositor;
  `App::title` is read again after every rebuild and sent when it changes.
- **A refusal is read from the kernel.** `appkit::Refusal` works out why a
  file was refused from `statfs` and `FS_IOC_GETFLAGS`. A path says nothing
  reliable about which filesystem a file is on, so never decide from it.
- **Icons are named, never embedded.** `theme-core::Icon` names each glyph
  of `/usr/share/fonts/Lucide.ttf`, and the UI text renderer draws it at a
  text role's size.
- **Frames and the dock are drawn by hand, from the same tokens.** They are
  painted every frame, so they use the compositor's own drawing code. A
  frame's rounded edge takes its coverage from the signed distance
  `gfx::rounded` computes, which is why the shadow follows the corners. The
  wallpaper is shaded by `theme-core::Shading`, one per screen size, over
  the theme's `GRADIENTS` (`compositor::wallpaper`), and a picture is placed
  by `settings-core::background::places`. The dock draws the 64 px icon and
  never larger, because its filter samples two pixels a side; the 128 px
  one waits for a scale factor.
- **Where a window opens is decided off screen.** `chrome-core::placement`
  takes the area, the window's size and least size and the app's latest
  window, and its tests run on the build machine. An app's windows are
  known by app id, or by client when a window has none. A window shrunk to
  fit hears its new size as a configure, and is never made smaller than the
  floor a resize keeps. `chrome-core::clock` formats the time and keeps the
  menu bar's width for the widest time, so the bar doesn't shift each
  minute.
- **The System Monitor's arithmetic is separate from its sampling.** The
  histories and rates live in a `Monitor` (`monitor.rs`) that makes no
  system call, so a test feeds it samples of its own. Each disk counts its
  own activity for `block_stats`, apart from the machine-wide counters in
  `slopos_fs::blockdev::stats`, which tests reset.

## Further reading

- [Understanding SC 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum)](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)

  How GNOME's toolkit names its colours by surface and by meaning, the model

  The icon rules `tools/icons` follows.
- [GNOME HIG: Writing style](https://developer.gnome.org/hig/guidelines/writing-style.html).
  Short, plain interface text and when a label takes an ellipsis.
- [*The Elm Architecture*](https://guide.elm-lang.org/architecture/). The
  model, view and update split appkit's `App` uses.
- [Design Tokens Format Module](https://www.designtokens.org/tr/2025.10/format/).
  The cross-tool vocabulary for named design values like `theme-core`'s.
- [Lucide's licence](https://lucide.dev/license). The icon font's terms, ISC
  with MIT for the icons derived from Feather.
